## 1. The § 2255(e) Savings Clause: When Does § 2241 Actually Work Instead?
*Post-Conviction · § 2241 · ~33s · 83 words*

**HOOK**  
Filing under the wrong statute can get your case thrown out before a judge reads a word of it.

**BODY**  
Federal prisoners must normally use § 2255 to challenge a conviction. The § 2255(e) savings clause allows a § 2241 petition instead only when § 2255 is 'inadequate or ineffective' — a narrow exception.

**DETAIL**  
After Jones v. Hendrix (2023), a new statutory-interpretation argument does not qualify. Execution-of-sentence claims still belong in § 2241.

## 3. AEDPA Deference: Why 'The State Court Got It Wrong' Isn't Enough for a § 2254 Petition
*Post-Conviction · § 2254 · ~33s · 82 words*

**HOOK**  
A federal judge can believe the state court got your case wrong and still have to deny you.

**BODY**  
Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), a federal court cannot grant habeas relief on a claim the state court decided on the merits unless that decision was contrary to, or an unreasonable application of, clearly established Supreme Court law, or rested on an unreasonable determination of the facts. Ordinary legal error is not enough.

**BODY**

*Post-Conviction · § 2254 · ~32s · 81 words*

**HOOK**  
A missed procedural step can end a strong claim before anyone looks at whether you're right.

**BODY**  
Procedural default means a federal court will not review a claim the state court refused to hear because you did not follow the state's procedural rules. It can be overcome by showing cause for the default plus prejudice, or by passing through the actual-innocence gateway.

**DETAIL**  
Ordinary attorney error usually does not qualify as cause.
