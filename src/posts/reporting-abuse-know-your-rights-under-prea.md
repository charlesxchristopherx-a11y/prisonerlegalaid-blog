Sexual abuse in custody is not something you have to accept as part of being incarcerated, and it's not something you have to handle alone or in silence. The Prison Rape Elimination Act, usually just called PREA, was passed in 2003 to set national standards for how facilities prevent, detect, and respond to sexual abuse of people in custody. It applies broadly — prisons, jails, juvenile facilities, and other detention settings — and it exists because, for a long time, there was no consistent baseline requiring facilities to take this seriously at all. PREA changed that, at least on paper, and understanding what it actually requires can help you know what you're entitled to if you ever need to use it.

One of the most important things PREA requires is that facilities provide more than one way to report abuse. That matters enormously, because if the only way to report is through the person who's alleged to have done something wrong, or through their direct supervisor, most people would understandably never report at all. PREA standards call for reporting paths that don't run through the person being reported — this can include outside hotlines, third-party organizations, medical or mental health staff, or other designated channels separate from the normal chain of command. If your facility only offers you one option and that option involves telling the person you're reporting or someone closely tied to them, that's a sign the reporting structure isn't meeting the standard, and it's worth knowing that alternatives are supposed to exist.

Retaliation is addressed directly, and this part deserves to be said plainly: PREA specifically prohibits retaliation against anyone who reports abuse, whether the report is about something that happened to them or something they witnessed happening to someone else. That includes retaliation from staff and is meant to include protection related to retaliation from other incarcerated people to the extent the facility can address it. Facilities are expected to monitor for retaliation after a report is made — things like sudden disciplinary write-ups, transfers, loss of privileges, or housing changes that follow closely after a report and don't have another clear explanation. If retaliation happens, that itself is something worth documenting and reporting through the same channels, because it's treated as its own violation, not just an unfortunate side effect.

It helps to understand why the standards are built this way. Fear of not being believed, fear of retaliation, and fear of having to report to the very system connected to the person who caused harm are exactly the barriers that keep abuse hidden and unaddressed. PREA's standards — multiple reporting paths, staff training requirements, protection from retaliation, access to victim advocates or support services where available — are designed to lower those barriers, not eliminate them entirely, because no set of paper standards can do that on its own. Knowing the standards exist is still worth something, because it gives you language and expectations to point to if a facility isn't meeting them.

Consider, in the abstract, how this might play out at a facility. Say a facility's only posted reporting method is a form that goes into a housing unit box checked by unit officers. Someone wants to report an incident involving a staff member, but doesn't trust that the form will reach anyone outside that officer's chain of command. Under PREA standards, that facility is expected to also offer something like a confidential hotline, an outside agency contact, or another channel that bypasses the unit staff entirely. If no such alternative is posted or available, that's a gap between what's required and what's actually being offered — and it's worth asking about directly, in writing if possible, or through a family member or advocate on the outside if that feels safer.

A common misconception is that reporting only "counts" if you use whatever form or process staff hand you first. That's not accurate — PREA is built around the idea that you should have options, and using an outside hotline, a medical provider, a chaplain, or an advocacy organization can all be legitimate ways to report, depending on what your facility has set up. Another misconception is that a report has to be about something that already happened to you personally. Reporting abuse you witnessed happening to someone else is also protected, and it matters — a lot of the information that leads to real change comes from people willing to speak up about what they saw, not just what they experienced.

If you do report, documentation still matters, even here, even though this isn't about building a paper trail the way a grievance might be. Note the date you reported, who you reported to or which method you used, and if you're comfortable doing so, whether you received any acknowledgment or follow-up. If you experience anything that feels like retaliation afterward, note the date and what happened, as specifically as you can. You don't have to do this alone or immediately — there's no requirement that you document everything perfectly in the moment. The point is just that if you ever want support in addressing what happened, having some record of dates and details helps, whenever you're ready to use it.

It's worth saying clearly that using these reporting channels is your right, not a burden you have to justify. You don't need to have all the language right, or know every standard by name, to report something that happened to you or that you saw happen to someone else. The system is supposed to meet you partway — with multiple ways in, protection from retaliation, and people whose job is to respond. Where it doesn't meet that standard, that's worth naming too, and there are people and organizations outside the facility who can help you push for that.
