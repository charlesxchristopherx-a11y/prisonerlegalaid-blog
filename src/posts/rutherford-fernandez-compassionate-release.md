On May 28, 2026, the Supreme Court decided two compassionate release cases the same day — Rutherford v. United States and Fernandez v. United States. Together, they close off two arguments federal prisoners had increasingly relied on since the First Step Act made compassionate release motions available directly to the courts. Compassionate release itself survives. These two specific theories don't.

## Rutherford: a nonretroactive law change is not, by itself, "extraordinary and compelling"

The First Step Act eliminated the "stacking" of certain § 924(c) firearm sentences — but Congress didn't make that change retroactive. Prisoners sentenced under the old stacking rule began arguing that the resulting gap between their sentence and what someone would receive today was itself an extraordinary and compelling reason for a sentence reduction, especially when combined with other factors.

The Supreme Court rejected that theory. The Court held that when Congress declines to make a sentencing amendment retroactive, the disparity that creates cannot serve as an extraordinary and compelling reason — not standing alone, and not in combination with other circumstances. The Court's reasoning: non-retroactivity is a deliberate congressional choice, not an oversight, and letting courts use § 3582(c)(1)(A) to undo that choice case-by-case would override the very judgment Congress made when it decided the law wouldn't apply retroactively. This also invalidated the part of the Sentencing Commission's 2023 policy statement that had allowed courts to weigh a nonretroactive change as one factor among several.

**What this means:** if the strongest thing in your motion is "the law changed after I was sentenced and it isn't retroactive," that argument no longer works — even paired with other circumstances.

## Fernandez: compassionate release can't be used to attack the conviction itself

The second case addressed a different kind of motion: prisoners arguing that their conviction or sentence was legally invalid, and asking the court to treat that as an "extraordinary and compelling reason" for release under § 3582(c)(1)(A) instead of going through the normal channel for challenging a conviction.

The Court held that's not what compassionate release is for. A claim that a conviction or sentence is unlawful has to proceed under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 (or another legally authorized avenue), not as a compassionate release motion. Compassionate release addresses circumstances that arose after sentencing — it isn't a side door around the procedural rules, deadlines, and gatekeeping requirements that govern challenges to the conviction itself.

**What this means:** if what you actually have is a claim that your conviction or sentence was unlawful, compassionate release is the wrong vehicle. That claim needs to go in a § 2255 motion, filed within its own procedural rules and deadlines.

## What still works after these decisions

Rutherford and Fernandez narrow two specific theories. They don't touch the core grounds compassionate release has always covered:

- **Serious medical conditions** — including terminal illness and conditions that substantially limit self-care in custody
- **Advanced age** combined with deteriorating health and time already served
- **Qualifying family circumstances** — most commonly the death or incapacitation of the only available caregiver for a minor child, or an incapacitated spouse
- Other individualized circumstances of similar gravity, evaluated on their own facts

The Court also left open questions it didn't decide — including whether other kinds of post-judgment developments, distinct from a nonretroactive statute, could still support a motion. That means the analysis is fact-specific, and a motion built on the wrong theory after these decisions is now an easy denial, not just a longshot.
