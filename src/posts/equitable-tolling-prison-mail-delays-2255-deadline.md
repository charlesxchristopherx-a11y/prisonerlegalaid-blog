Missing the one-year deadline under 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f) usually ends a case before a court ever looks at the merits. But "usually" isn't "always." Two doctrines — equitable tolling and the prisoner mailbox rule — exist specifically because Congress and the courts recognized that people in custody don't control their own mail the way someone on the outside does.

## The deadline itself, precisely

Section 2255(f) sets a one-year limitation period on "a motion under this section" — meaning a § 2255 motion to vacate, set aside, or correct a federal sentence. That one-year clock runs from the latest of four trigger dates, most commonly the date the judgment of conviction became final. This deadline applies specifically to § 2255 motions. It does not govern § 2241 petitions, which have no equivalent statutory deadline, and it does not govern compassionate-release motions under 18 U.S.C. § 3582(c)(1)(A), which are gated by administrative exhaustion rather than a filing clock. Confusing the three is one of the more common — and costly — mistakes people make when they're researching their own case.

## The two-part test for equitable tolling

The Supreme Court laid out the controlling standard in *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631 (2010): a petitioner is entitled to equitable tolling only if he shows **(1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way** and prevented timely filing.

Both parts matter, and courts apply them strictly. "Diligence" in this context means *reasonable* diligence — not maximum, superhuman effort — but it does mean you can show what you actually did to try to file on time. An extraordinary circumstance is something outside your control: a facility-wide lockdown that cuts off law-library access, a lost or destroyed legal file during a transfer, or — directly relevant here — a documented, unreasonable mail delay by prison staff that ate into your filing window despite your own timely efforts.

Vague claims of "the mail was slow" rarely clear this bar on their own. What tends to matter is a documented timeline: when you handed the motion to staff for mailing, what response (if any) you got when you followed up, and whether the delay was ordinary processing or something genuinely unusual.

## The prisoner mailbox rule: your filing date isn't the court's receipt date

Separately, and often underused, is the rule from *Houston v. Lack*, 487 U.S. 266 (1988): for a pro se prisoner, a filing is deemed submitted **the moment it's delivered to prison staff for forwarding to the court** — not whenever the court clerk happens to receive and stamp it. The Court's reasoning was practical: once you hand a filing to prison staff, you lose all further control over it. You can't personally walk it to the courthouse, track it, or expedite it. The date on the prison's own outgoing-mail log (or a certified mail receipt, if you have access to one) is the date that counts.

This matters even outside a tolling fight. If your motion sat in an outgoing mail bin for two weeks before it was actually postmarked, the mailbox rule — not the postmark, and not the court's stamp — is what should control your filing date, provided you can document when you handed it over.

## What this looks like in practice

If you believe mail delay affected your deadline, the pieces worth preserving are specific: the date you completed and handed over your motion, any request slips or cop-outs you submitted about outgoing legal mail, any responses (or lack of response) from staff, and whatever your facility's own mail log shows. Equitable tolling is an exception courts apply narrowly, and it's decided on the specific record you can put in front of the judge — not on a general sense that the system was slow.
