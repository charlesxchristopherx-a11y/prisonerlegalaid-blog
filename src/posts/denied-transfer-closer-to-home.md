He is eight hours away and the answer came back no.

The hard part first, because someone will say it to you sooner or later. No law gives him a right to any particular prison, and Congress wrote into the statute that the choice of prison is not something a court will review.

The part almost nobody tells families sits in the same statute. It tells the Bureau to put him close to home, to move him closer even when he is already within 500 driving miles, and to weigh what he himself wants. A denial is not the end of that. It is the start of a paper record, and you can begin building it today.

## What the statute actually tells them to do

Here is the first sentence of 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b):

> "The Bureau of Prisons shall designate the place of the prisoner's imprisonment, and shall, subject to bed availability, the prisoner's security designation, the prisoner's programmatic needs, the prisoner's mental and medical health needs, any request made by the prisoner related to faith-based needs, recommendations of the sentencing court, and other security concerns of the Bureau of Prisons, place the prisoner in a facility as close as practicable to the prisoner's primary residence, and to the extent practicable, in a facility within 500 driving miles of that residence."

And the sentence right after it:

> "The Bureau shall, subject to consideration of the factors described in the preceding sentence and the prisoner's preference for staying at his or her current facility or being transferred, transfer prisoners to facilities that are closer to the prisoner's primary residence even if the prisoner is already in a facility within 500 driving miles of that residence."

Read that first list twice. Those seven items are the only reasons the statute gives for keeping him where he is. A denial that names none of them is worth asking about in writing. A denial that names one tells you which document you need next.

The same subsection also says the Bureau "may at any time, having regard for the same matters, direct the transfer of a prisoner from one penal or correctional facility to another." Nothing about where he sits today locks him there.

If he has children, a second provision is worth naming by number. Under 18 U.S.C. § 4051(b)(2), the office inside the Bureau that determines placement shall, "if the prisoner has children, consider placing the prisoner as close to the children as possible." Put their names and ages in the paperwork. A file that never mentions them cannot be weighed.

## Now the hard half, in the words that will be used on you

The last sentence of § 3621(b) is the one that matters most:

> "Notwithstanding any other provision of law, a designation of a place of imprisonment under this subsection is not reviewable by any court."

The Supreme Court has said the same about the prison itself. In *Olim v. Wakinekona*, 461 U.S. 238 (1983), the Court held that "Just as an inmate has no justifiable expectation that he will be incarcerated in any particular prison within a State, he has no justifiable expectation that he will be incarcerated in any particular State." In *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215 (1976), it described an expectation of remaining at one prison as "too ephemeral and insubstantial to trigger procedural due process protections as long as prison officials have discretion to transfer him for whatever reason or for no reason at all."

Both cases were about state prisoners moved against their will, not a federal prisoner asking to be brought closer. They are here because they are why no judge will order this move, and a rougher version of them will be quoted at you.

So this gets won inside the Bureau, in writing. If the real reason you want him nearer is that he is seriously ill, that is a different filing under a different statute, and [we wrote that one up on its own](/blog/compassionate-release-3582/).

## What he puts in writing this week

The route is the Administrative Remedy Program, and it starts informally. Under 28 C.F.R. § 542.13(a), "an inmate shall first present an issue of concern informally to staff, and staff shall attempt to informally resolve the issue before an inmate submits a Request for Administrative Remedy." Inside, that is the BP-8 and a written request to his unit team.

Three things belong in it. Ask for the denial in writing. Ask which factor in § 3621(b) it rests on. Name the facility he wants and the driving miles to it. Keep his copy.

After that the formal filings, and they run on short clocks.

- **BP-9 to the warden.** The deadline "for completion of informal resolution and submission of a formal written Administrative Remedy Request, on the appropriate form (BP-9), is 20 calendar days following the date on which the basis for the Request occurred." 28 C.F.R. § 542.14(a). Calendar days, not business days, counted from the denial.
- **Already past the twenty days?** He files anyway. Section 542.14(b) allows an extension where the inmate "demonstrates a valid reason for delay," and lists examples, among them "an unusually long period taken for informal resolution attempts." Write the reason out.
- **BP-10, then BP-11.** Under 28 C.F.R. § 542.15(a), an inmate not satisfied with the warden's answer "may submit an Appeal on the appropriate form (BP-10) to the appropriate Regional Director within 20 calendar days of the date the Warden signed the response," and then a BP-11 "to the General Counsel within 30 calendar days of the date the Regional Director signed the response."
- **Silence counts as an answer.** 28 C.F.R. § 542.18 gives the warden 20 calendar days, the Regional Director 30 and the General Counsel 40, with one written extension. Then this: "If the inmate does not receive a response within the time allotted for reply, including extension, the inmate may consider the absence of a response to be a denial at that level."

One more thing, and it shapes your role. He signs and submits them himself: "The inmate shall date and sign the Request and submit it to the institution staff member designated to receive such Requests." 28 C.F.R. § 542.14(c)(4). You cannot file this one for him, so the envelope you send is where you matter. If you want a second set of eyes on the file first, [request a free case review](/#contact).

## Your half, and it usually decides it

He cannot reach any of this from inside. Put it together this week and mail it to him by certified mail.

- **Proof of the primary residence.** A lease, a mortgage statement, a utility bill with the address on it. The statute turns on his "primary residence," so the file has to show it.
- **Who lives at it.** Children's names and ages, birth certificates, a letter from a school. That is the material § 4051(b)(2) asks the placement office to consider.
- **The miles.** Print the driving distance from that address to his current facility and to the one he is asking for, both numbers on one page. Nobody at the Bureau will look them up.
- **Who cannot make the trip, and why.** A letter from a doctor about someone on the outside, a note about a grandmother who does not drive, a work schedule that makes a two day round trip impossible.
- **Anything the judge said.** "Recommendations of the sentencing court" is one of the listed factors. If the judge said anything at sentencing about where he should serve it, that language is in the judgment or the transcript.

Keep a copy of every page you send him. Months from now a regional office may be reading nothing but the record the two of you made.

## Traps that cost people the filing

- One subject per form. Where an inmate puts multiple unrelated issues on one form, Section 542.14(c)(2) says "the submission shall be rejected and returned without response." Bundled with an unrelated complaint, a transfer request can come back untouched while the clock runs.
- Nothing new on appeal. Section 542.15(b)(2): "An inmate may not raise in an Appeal issues not raised in the lower level filings." Everything you want considered has to be in the BP-9 the first time.
- The designation itself is not reviewable by any court, so the administrative record is the whole of this fight.

## Checklist

- Ask him to put a written transfer request to his unit team in this week, and keep his copy.
- Ask him to ask, in writing, which § 3621(b) factor the denial rests on.
- Put the twenty calendar day BP-9 date on a calendar you see every morning.
- Gather proof of the primary residence and of who lives at it, children included.
- Print the driving miles to his current facility and to the one he wants, on one page.
- Collect the letters showing who on the outside cannot travel, and why.
- Check the judgment and the sentencing transcript for anything the judge said about placement.
- Mail it by certified mail and keep a copy of every page.
