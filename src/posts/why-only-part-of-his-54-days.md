Somebody told your husband he gets 54 days a year off his federal sentence for staying out of trouble. Then the paperwork came back with a smaller number, and nobody explained the gap. Here is the short answer. The 54 days is a rate, not a lump sum, and the Bureau of Prisons prorates the last partial year of a sentence instead of awarding a full 54 days for it. That alone accounts for most of the shortfalls families call us about. There are two other ordinary explanations underneath it, and you can check all three yourself this week from two pieces of paper.

## What to ask him for this week

Start with the documents, not the argument. Ask him to put in a written request to his unit team, on an inmate request to staff form, for two things: his **Sentence Monitoring Computation Data** sheet, and his **First Step Act Time Credit Assessment**. These are the two records that show how his number was built. Ask him to keep a copy of the request he turns in and to date it.

From the computation sheet, write down three figures and keep them where you can find them: the total sentence the judge imposed, the date the sentence commenced, and the current projected release date. Those three numbers are what any later conversation runs on.

If you mail him anything you want a record of, send it certified mail and keep the receipt.

If the numbers look wrong once you have them in front of you, you can [request a free case review](/#contact) and we will read them with you.

## The 54 days is a rate for each year, so the last piece is smaller

The statute is 18 U.S.C. 3624(b)(1). It says a prisoner serving more than a year, other than a life term, "may receive credit toward the service of the prisoner's sentence of up to 54 days for each year of the prisoner's sentence imposed by the court, subject to determination by the Bureau of Prisons that, during that year, the prisoner has displayed exemplary compliance with institutional disciplinary regulations." The same paragraph adds a sentence worth reading twice: "Credit that has not been earned may not later be granted."

The Bureau's own regulation says the same thing and then says what happens to the leftover months. Under 28 C.F.R. 523.20(b)(1), "The Bureau will award inmates up to 54 days of GCT credit for each year of the sentence imposed by the court." Under 523.20(b)(2), "The Bureau will award prorated credit for any partial final year of the sentence imposed."

Prorated means divided down, and a sentence almost never lands on a clean number of whole years.

In October 2025 the Third Circuit decided this exact fight. A man with a seventeen and a half year sentence argued he should get a full 54 days for his last six months. The court recorded what the agency had done: "the BOP calculated that the prisoner would be eligible for 26, not 54, days of credit for the last six months of his sentence." It held that "the revised statute is most naturally read as setting a rate for good conduct time credit at 54 days per year," and affirmed. See Thieme v. Warden Fort Dix FCI, No. 23-1697 (3d Cir. Oct. 8, 2025).

That ruling binds federal courts in the Third Circuit and nowhere else. What matters for your family either way is that the Bureau uses this method nationwide, so a short final piece is normal rather than a mistake.

## The second reason: 54 or 42, and a diploma decides it

This one catches people, and it is fixable from inside.

For offenses committed on or after April 26, 1996, 28 C.F.R. 523.20(d)(2) sets two different rates. The Bureau awards "Up to 54 days of GCT credit for each year of the sentence imposed ... if the inmate has earned or is making satisfactory progress toward earning a high school diploma, equivalent degree, or Bureau-authorized alternative program credit," and "Up to 42 days of GCT credit for each year of the sentence imposed ... if the inmate does not meet conditions described in paragraph (d)(2)(i)."

Twelve days a year is a real number over a long sentence. If he does not have a diploma or its equivalent and is not enrolled in the program, that is the difference, and enrolling is the whole fix.

The same regulation says at 523.20(a) that this credit "may be reduced" if an inmate "Commits prohibited acts which result in certain disciplinary sanctions" or "Fails to comply with literacy requirements." If he has had a write up, read our guide on [what happens when they take credits back](/blog/credits-taken-after-one-write-up/).

## The third reason: First Step Act credits are a different program

Good conduct time and First Step Act time credits are two separate systems with separate rules, and families, and sometimes staff, slide between them. Keeping them apart is most of what it takes to read his paperwork correctly.

First Step Act credits are earned by programming, not by behaviour alone. Under 18 U.S.C. 3632(d)(4)(A), "A prisoner shall earn 10 days of time credits for every 30 days of successful participation in evidence-based recidivism reduction programming or productive activities," and a prisoner the Bureau rates at minimum or low risk who "over 2 consecutive assessments, has not increased their risk of recidivism, shall earn an additional 5 days of time credits for every 30 days." The regulation at 28 C.F.R. 523.42(c) repeats both rates.

28 C.F.R. 523.41(c)(4) lists what stalls that count. A person is generally not treated as "successfully participating" while he is in a Special Housing Unit, designated outside the institution, on temporary transfer to another agency, on a mental health hold, or opted out. Opting out stops further First Step Act benefits under (c)(5)(iii) "until the date the inmate 'opts in'." If his count went flat for a stretch, one of those is usually why.

A conviction on the list in 18 U.S.C. 3632(d)(4)(D) means he is not eligible to earn these credits at all. That list controls earning. It is not the same as the separate rules on how credits already earned get used, which are at 28 C.F.R. 523.44.

And what the credits buy is limited. Under 18 U.S.C. 3632(d)(4)(C) they "shall be applied toward time in prerelease custody or supervised release." Under 18 U.S.C. 3624(g)(3), the Director "may transfer the prisoner to begin any such term of supervised release at an earlier date, not to exceed 12 months." That is permission, not a command, and twelve months is the ceiling. ## The clocks, if the math is actually wrong

If the computation sheet shows something that cannot be right, there is a formal route and it is short on time.

He raises it informally with staff first, because 28 C.F.R. 542.13(a) says an inmate "shall first present an issue of concern informally to staff."

Then the BP-9. Under 28 C.F.R. 542.14(a), "The deadline for completion of informal resolution and submission of a formal written Administrative Remedy Request, on the appropriate form (BP-9), is 20 calendar days following the date on which the basis for the Request occurred." Twenty calendar days. That is the clock that costs people their issue.

Then the appeals. Under 28 C.F.R. 542.15(a), a BP-10 goes to the Regional Director "within 20 calendar days of the date the Warden signed the response," and a BP-11 goes to the General Counsel "within 30 calendar days of the date the Regional Director signed the response." The same paragraph says "Appeal to the General Counsel is the final administrative appeal."

Silence is not a dead end. 28 C.F.R. 542.18 gives the Warden 20 calendar days, the Regional Director 30 and the General Counsel 40, and says that "If the inmate does not receive a response within the time allotted for reply, including extension, the inmate may consider the absence of a response to be a denial at that level." So he moves to the next level rather than waiting.

## Your checklist

- Ask him this week for the Sentence Monitoring Computation Data sheet and the First Step Act Time Credit Assessment, in writing.
- Copy down the sentence imposed, the date it commenced and the projected release date.
- Check whether his good conduct time is running at the 54 day rate or the 42 day rate, and whether he is enrolled in the diploma program.
- Look for flat stretches in the First Step Act count and match them against the dates on the computation sheet.
- Keep every piece of paper, both the request and the answer, with dates.
- If a number is wrong, the BP-9 goes in within 20 calendar days of when the problem arose.
- If no answer comes back in time, treat it as a denial and go to the next level.
