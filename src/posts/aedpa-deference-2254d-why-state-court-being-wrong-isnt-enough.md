One of the hardest things to accept about § 2254 habeas review is this: a federal court can privately believe the state court got your case wrong and still be legally required to deny your petition. That isn't a quirk of one unsympathetic judge — it's the deference standard Congress wrote directly into the statute.

## The exact standard, in the statute's own words

Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), a federal court cannot grant habeas relief on any claim the state court already decided on the merits **unless** that state-court decision:

1. "resulted in a decision that was contrary to, or involved an unreasonable application of, clearly established Federal law, as determined by the Supreme Court of the United States," or
2. "resulted in a decision that was based on an unreasonable determination of the facts in light of the evidence presented in the State court proceeding."

Notice what's *not* in that list: "the state court was wrong." Ordinary legal error — even error a federal judge is confident about — doesn't satisfy either prong on its own.

## "Contrary to" versus "unreasonable application" — they're different tests

These two paths under prong one are not interchangeable. A decision is **"contrary to"** clearly established federal law if the state court applied a rule that directly conflicts with governing Supreme Court precedent, or reached the opposite result on materially indistinguishable facts. A decision involves an **"unreasonable application"** of federal law if the state court identified the correct governing rule but applied it to the facts of the case in an objectively unreasonable way — not just an incorrect way. Courts have repeatedly stressed that "unreasonable" here means something well beyond "wrong": it has to be a application so far off that no fair-minded jurist could agree with it.

## "Clearly established federal law" has a specific, narrow meaning

This phrase doesn't mean "the law as a federal court would apply it today." It means the holdings — not dicta — of the U.S. Supreme Court, as they existed **at the time of the relevant state-court decision.** A state court cannot be faulted under § 2254(d) for failing to anticipate a Supreme Court ruling that came later, and a federal habeas court generally cannot rely on circuit-court precedent alone to satisfy this prong — it has to trace back to the Supreme Court itself.

## Why this matters before you file

This standard is the reason so much of § 2254 practice is about *finding the right Supreme Court precedent* and showing precisely how the state court's reasoning departed from it — not simply re-arguing the underlying facts of the case as if it were a direct appeal. A petition that reads like "here's why I should have won at trial" is answering the wrong question. A petition built around AEDPA deference is answering: *what specific, clearly established Supreme Court rule did the state court get contrary to, or unreasonably apply — and why is that not just wrong, but unreasonable?*

Understanding this distinction early tends to shape which claims are worth developing in depth, and which ones — however emotionally central to the case — simply don't have a Supreme Court hook strong enough to clear § 2254(d)'s bar.
