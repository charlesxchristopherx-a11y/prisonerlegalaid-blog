He finished the programs. His counselor said the date would move. You checked the locator this morning and it is the same date it has always been.

Here is the answer. Earning First Step Act credits and getting them applied to his release are two separate steps, and the second step has conditions the first one does not. A date that will not move is usually one of three things: credits he earned that cannot be applied yet, programming the Bureau never recorded as successful participation, or a computation error nobody has been asked in writing to fix. All three get answered by paper. This week he can request his sentence computation in writing and file a BP-9 naming the exact credits he believes are missing. That puts a clock on them instead of on him.

## What he can do this week

Ask him to request his sentence computation in writing from his case manager. Not hallway talk. The printed sheet. Until the two of you are looking at the same document, you are arguing about a number neither of you has seen.

Then ask him for his last two risk and needs assessments. Those two pieces of paper decide more about his date than anything else in his file.

Then he files, and he has to be the one who does it. The Bureau's regulation says you may help, then says plainly that you cannot send it: "An inmate may also obtain assistance from outside sources, such as family members or attorneys. However, no person may submit a Request or Appeal on the inmate's behalf." 28 C.F.R. § 542.16(a). So you gather, you type, you mail him copies by certified mail, you keep the calendar. He signs and submits. Anyone who says they will file it for him is confused or selling you something.

## What the law actually requires

**He earns credits by the month, at a rate the statute fixes.** Under 18 U.S.C. § 3632(d)(4)(A), "A prisoner shall earn 10 days of time credits for every 30 days of successful participation in evidence-based recidivism reduction programming or productive activities," and a prisoner the Bureau rates at minimum or low risk who, "over 2 consecutive assessments, has not increased their risk of recidivism, shall earn an additional 5 days of time credits for every 30 days." The regulation says the same in plain days. 28 C.F.R. § 523.42(c).

**"Successful participation" is a staff determination, not a certificate.** This is where many frozen dates live. The regulation defines it as "a determination by Bureau staff that an eligible inmate has participated in the EBRR programs or PAs that the Bureau has recommended based on the inmate's individualized risk and needs assessment, and has complied with the requirements of each particular EBRR Program or PA." 28 C.F.R. § 523.41(c)(2). A class he found on his own and finished with pride may not be one the Bureau recommended for him. Ask about that in writing before anything else.

**Earned is not applied.** Credits "shall be applied toward time in prerelease custody or supervised release." 18 U.S.C. § 3632(d)(4)(C). They do not come off the back end of the sentence on their own. Before the Bureau may apply them, 18 U.S.C. § 3624(g)(1) sets conditions that 28 C.F.R. § 523.44(b) restates in the Bureau's own words: he must have "Earned FSA Time Credits in an amount that is equal to the remainder of the inmate's imposed term of imprisonment," have "Shown through the periodic risk reassessments a demonstrated recidivism risk reduction or maintained a minimum or low recidivism risk," and have "Had the remainder of his or her imposed term of imprisonment computed under applicable law." So he can earn credits every month and watch the public date sit still, because the date that moves first is his halfway house or [home confinement](/blog/bop-camp-transfer-before-rrc-home-confinement/) date.

**The risk assessments are the gate.** For prerelease custody, the regulation requires that he have "Maintained a minimum or low recidivism risk through his or her last two risk and needs assessments," or that the Warden approve a petition after determining that he would not be a danger to society, has made a good faith effort to lower his risk, and is unlikely to recidivate. 28 C.F.R. § 523.44(c). For early transfer to supervised release the last assessment must be minimum or low, he must have a term of supervised release in his judgment, and the move may be "no earlier than 12 months before the date that transfer to supervised release would otherwise have occurred." 28 C.F.R. § 523.44(d). The statute sets the same ceiling: the Director "may transfer the prisoner to begin any such term of supervised release at an earlier date, not to exceed 12 months." 18 U.S.C. § 3624(g)(3). May, not must.

**A write-up can cost credits, and what it can cost is limited.** If credits disappeared after a [disciplinary report](/blog/can-you-sue-over-a-wrongful-disciplinary-write-up/), any reduction "shall require written notice to the prisoner, shall be limited to time credits that a prisoner earned as of the date of the prisoner's rule violation, and shall not include any future time credits that the prisoner may earn." 18 U.S.C. § 3632(e)(2). The same subsection requires "a procedure to restore time credits that a prisoner lost as a result of a rule violation," and the regulation says part or all may be restored, case by case, after clear conduct "for two consecutive risk and needs assessments conducted by the Bureau." 28 C.F.R. § 523.43(c). Ask for the written notice. If there is none, say so on the form.

**Good conduct time is a separate system.** The 54 day figure families hear about is not First Step Act credit. "The Bureau will award inmates up to 54 days of GCT credit for each year of the sentence imposed by the court," and that credit "may be reduced" if an inmate "Commits prohibited acts which result in certain disciplinary sanctions" or "Fails to comply with literacy requirements." 28 C.F.R. § 523.20(a), (b)(1). Two systems, two reasons a date can be wrong. His written request should name both, so nobody answers half of it. The longer version: [how First Step Act time credits work](/blog/first-step-act-time-credits-explained/).

**Writing the judge will not fix this.** The Supreme Court settled that in United States v. Wilson, 503 U.S. 329 (1992). The statute "does not authorize a district court to compute the credit at sentencing," because "the Attorney General has no choice but to make the determination as an administrative matter when imprisoning the defendant." Letters to chambers reach the one person who cannot change it.

## The deadlines, and the trap inside them

The administrative remedy is available to him, and it runs on short clocks nobody inside will remind him about.

First, informal resolution with his counselor. Then the formal request. This is the deadline that matters most. "The deadline for completion of informal resolution and submission of a formal written Administrative Remedy Request, on the appropriate form (BP-9), is 20 calendar days following the date on which the basis for the Request occurred." 28 C.F.R. § 542.14(a). Twenty days, not thirty. If he is already past it, § 542.14(b) allows an extension "Where the inmate demonstrates a valid reason for delay," so he files anyway and explains the delay on the form.

Then the appeals. A BP-10 to the Regional Director goes in "within 20 calendar days of the date the Warden signed the response," and a BP-11 to the General Counsel "within 30 calendar days of the date the Regional Director signed the response." 28 C.F.R. § 542.15(a). That same section adds that "Appeal to the General Counsel is the final administrative appeal." That matters, because a credit dispute that has to go further than the Bureau generally goes after this ladder is finished: [what happens after you exhaust administrative remedies](/blog/what-happens-after-you-exhaust-administrative-remedies/).

Now the trap. Silence is not the end. "If the inmate does not receive a response within the time allotted for reply, including extension, the inmate may consider the absence of a response to be a denial at that level." 28 C.F.R. § 542.18. The Warden has 20 calendar days, the Regional Director 30 and the General Counsel 40, with one written extension allowed. When nobody answers, he does not wait. He appeals from the silence, inside the next window.

One issue per form. Keep it to the date and nothing else.

## His checklist

- Request the sentence computation sheet and his last two risk and needs assessments in writing, and keep copies.
- Ask in writing which programs the Bureau recommended, and which it recorded as successful participation.
- Ask whether any credits were taken, and for the written notice 18 U.S.C. § 3632(e)(2) requires.
- File the BP-9 within 20 calendar days of the date the problem arose, one issue only.
- Have him mail you a copy of every form the day he files it.
- Calendar the response dates: 20 days, then 30, then 40. Treat silence as a denial and appeal.
