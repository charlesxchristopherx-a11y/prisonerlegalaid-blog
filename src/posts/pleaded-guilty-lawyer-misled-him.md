## The plea can be challenged, but the ground has to be the advice

A guilty plea is not the end of the road. A federal prisoner can ask the court that sentenced him to undo a conviction he pleaded to, and bad advice from his own lawyer is one of the grounds the courts recognize. What they will not do is reopen a plea because he changed his mind, or because the sentence came out worse than he hoped. The claim has to be that his lawyer's advice fell below what the Constitution requires, and that the bad advice is the reason he signed. The filing is a motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255, one of the [Section 2255 motions](/topics/2255-motions/) the sentencing court hears, and there is a one year clock on it. You can start building the record for it this week.

## What you can do this week

Start with the paper. Write to his trial lawyer and ask, in writing, for the complete file: the plea agreement, the discovery, the letters, the lawyer's notes. Ask the clerk of the sentencing court for the transcripts of the plea hearing and of the sentencing. Send your letters by certified mail and keep a copy of everything you send.

Then get his own account down while he still remembers it in detail. Have him write out, in his own words and signed, what his lawyer told him: the words themselves, the date, where the conversation happened, who else heard it, and what he understood he was agreeing to. If a relative or a friend sat in on a meeting, or heard the lawyer say it on the phone, have that person write and sign a short statement too. None of that is busy work. It is the difference between a claim a judge can act on and one he cannot.

If you want someone to read what you have gathered, [request a free case review](/#contact).

## What the law actually requires

Two things, and both have to be there.

The first is that the lawyer's work was not good enough. In Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), the Supreme Court held that "the defendant must show that counsel's representation fell below an objective standard of reasonableness." Courts are deliberately slow to find this. They start from the assumption that what the lawyer did was a reasonable professional choice, and that is [what the Strickland standard asks of him](/blog/ineffective-assistance-of-counsel-strickland-standard/).

The second is that the bad advice changed what he did. In Hill v. Lockhart, 474 U.S. 52 (1985), the Court applied Strickland to pleas and said what it means here: "the defendant must show that there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's errors, he would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on going to trial." A reasonable probability, Strickland explains, is "a probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the outcome." He does not have to prove he would have won at trial. He has to show he would have taken his chances.

Some kinds of misleading are squarely covered. In Missouri v. Frye, 566 U.S. 134 (2012), the Court held that "defense counsel has the duty to communicate formal offers from the prosecution to accept a plea on terms and conditions that may be favorable to the accused." A lawyer who let a written offer expire without telling him it existed did not do his job. And in Lee v. United States, 582 U.S. 357 (2017), the Court recognized that "the decision whether to plead guilty also involves assessing the respective consequences of a conviction after trial and by plea." Wrong advice about what the plea would actually cost him is not a side issue. It is the decision itself.

## The hard part, and why it is not the end

Here is what the government will say, and it is better to hear it from us first. At the plea hearing a judge asked him, on the record, whether anyone had promised him anything, and he answered no. Blackledge v. Allison, 431 U.S. 63 (1977) is blunt about the weight of that answer: "Solemn declarations in open court carry a strong presumption of verity."

That same case is the reason this is not over. The Supreme Court would not let the signed plea form be conclusive, holding that "the federal courts cannot fairly adopt a per se rule excluding all possibility that a defendant's representations at the time his guilty plea was accepted were so much the product of such factors as misunderstanding, duress, or misrepresentation by others as to make the guilty plea a constitutionally inadequate basis for imprisonment." What carried the day there was detail. The Court noted that the petition "indicated exactly what the terms of the promise were; when, where, and by whom the promise had been made; and the identity of one witness to its communication." That is the standard his own statement should try to meet.

The statute leans the same way. 28 U.S.C. § 2255(b) says that "unless the motion and the files and records of the case conclusively show that the prisoner is entitled to no relief," the court "shall cause notice thereof to be served upon the United States attorney, grant a prompt hearing thereon, determine the issues and make findings of fact and conclusions of law with respect thereto." A vague complaint gets nowhere. Specifics are what get a hearing.

## Deadlines and traps

The clock is the first thing to pin down. 28 U.S.C. § 2255 sets "a 1-year period of limitation," running from the latest of four dates. The one that applies in most cases is "the date on which the judgment of conviction becomes final." The others are the date an "impediment to making a motion created by governmental action" is removed, the date the Supreme Court "newly recognized" the right he is asserting if that right was also "made retroactively applicable to cases on collateral review," and "the date on which the facts supporting the claim or claims presented could have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence." If you think the year may already have run, that is a subject with its own rules and it is worth asking about rather than giving up on.

He does not lose this claim by having stayed quiet about it on appeal, which matters if [his appeal is already finished](/blog/husband-lost-his-appeal-is-it-over/). In Massaro v. United States, 538 U.S. 500 (2003), the Court held that "an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may be brought in a collateral proceeding under § 2255, whether or not the petitioner could have raised the claim on direct appeal."

No lawyer comes with the motion. 28 U.S.C. § 2255(g) says the court "may appoint counsel." May, not shall. Plan on filing it without one.

In practice he gets one attempt at this, so the first filing has to carry everything. A second or later motion has to be certified by a panel of the court of appeals, and 28 U.S.C. § 2255(h) allows that only for "newly discovered evidence" strong enough that "no reasonable factfinder would have found the movant guilty," or for "a new rule of constitutional law, made retroactive to cases on collateral review by the Supreme Court, that was previously unavailable."

## Your checklist

- Written request to his trial lawyer for the complete file, sent by certified mail.
- Transcripts of the plea hearing and the sentencing, from the clerk of the sentencing court.
- His own signed statement: the words, the date, the place, who else heard them.
- A signed statement from anyone else who heard what the lawyer said.
- The date his judgment of conviction became final, written down where you can find it.
- A copy of every letter and every plea offer in the file.
- A note of what he says he would have done if he had been told the truth.
