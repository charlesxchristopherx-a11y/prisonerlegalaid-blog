If staff have told you that your husband or your son does not get his First Step Act credits, the number on his paperwork is not the last word. These credits are set by statute, they are calculated by hand, and there is a formal process for fixing the count because the count goes wrong. What you can do this week is get two documents: his current sentence computation sheet and his FSA Time Credit Assessment. Nearly every one of these fights is won or lost on what those two pages say.

One distinction decides most of them, and families are told it wrong more often than anything else. Earning credits and being allowed to use them are two different questions with two different rule books. A man can be fully entitled to earn credits and still not be moved up. So when someone says "he does not get the credits," find out which of the two they mean.

## What you can do this week

Ask him to submit a written request to his unit team, on the inmate request form, for a printout of his FSA Time Credit Assessment and his current sentence computation. He should ask for the calculation itself, not just the final release date, and keep a copy of what he sent and the date. Ask him to request his Individualized Needs Plan from his last program review too, because that is the document listing what the Bureau recommended and what he finished.

Then do the arithmetic yourself. Count the thirty day periods of programming he completed and multiply. If his total is short, you have found the dispute. If his total looks right but his date has not moved, the dispute is about using the credits rather than earning them, which is a different argument.

If the number is wrong, he starts with the informal resolution form and then files the formal written request to the Warden. That second filing has a 20 calendar day deadline, which is the trap in this whole process. A request built on specific program names and specific dates is treated very differently from one that says the credits are wrong.

## What the law actually requires

The rate is in 18 U.S.C. 3632(d)(4)(A). Clause (i) says a prisoner "shall earn 10 days of time credits for every 30 days of successful participation in evidence-based recidivism reduction programming or productive activities." Clause (ii) adds that a prisoner the Bureau determines "to be at a minimum or low risk for recidivating, who, over 2 consecutive assessments, has not increased their risk of recidivism, shall earn an additional 5 days of time credits for every 30 days" of that participation. The Bureau's own regulation, 28 C.F.R. 523.42, repeats both numbers.

Who can earn them at all turns on his conviction. 18 U.S.C. 3632(d)(4)(D) says "A prisoner is ineligible to receive time credits under this paragraph if the prisoner is serving a sentence for a conviction under any of the following provisions of law," and then lists them. Short of that list, 28 C.F.R. 523.41 is broad: "Any inmate sentenced to a term of imprisonment pursuant to a conviction for a Federal criminal offense, or any person in the custody of the Bureau, is eligible to earn FSA Time Credits," subject to that one exception.

Using the credits is where families get surprised. 18 U.S.C. 3632(d)(4)(C) says credits earned by prisoners who successfully participate "shall be applied toward time in prerelease custody or supervised release," and that the Director "shall transfer eligible prisoners, as determined under section 3624(g)." But 18 U.S.C. 3624(g)(1) sets conditions, and they are real: he must have earned credits "in an amount that is equal to the remainder of the prisoner's imposed term of imprisonment," he must have shown reduced or steady low risk through the periodic reassessments, and the remainder of his term must have been computed under applicable law. Separately, 28 C.F.R. 523.44 says the Bureau may not apply credits at all for a man under a final order of removal, or serving a sentence for an offense under laws other than the U.S. Code.

There is also a ceiling. 18 U.S.C. 3624(g)(3) says the Director "may transfer the prisoner to begin any such term of supervised release at an earlier date, not to exceed 12 months." One court put the effect plainly: "A maximum of 365 days of FTCs may be applied towards a prisoner's early transfer to supervised release, basically shortening the sentence by up to one year." Herrera v. Rardin, No. 4:24-cv-11492 (E.D. Mich. Dec. 10, 2024). Credits past that go toward prerelease custody, meaning a reentry center or home confinement.

## Why the number stalls

The usual answer is not eligibility. It is the phrase "successful participation," which 28 C.F.R. 523.41 leaves to a staff determination. That regulation says a man is generally not treated as successfully participating during placement in a special housing unit, a designation outside the institution such as an extended hospital stay, a temporary transfer to another agency's custody, a mental health hold, or after he opts out of the programming the Bureau recommended. Those are months that quietly do not count, and nobody writes you a letter about them.

Start dates cause the rest. 28 C.F.R. 523.42 ties the start of earning to when his term of imprisonment commences. If his sheet starts the count on the day he arrived at his designated prison rather than the day his sentence began, name that gap in his filing with both dates spelled out.

Earned credits can also be taken. 28 C.F.R. 523.43 allows the loss of earned credits for violating the rules of a program or activity, following a disciplinary finding. The part families are not told is that the same regulation lets him ask for them back: part or all may be restored, case by case, after clear conduct for two consecutive risk and needs assessments. A loss is not always permanent.

## Deadlines and traps

The clocks are short, and they are the most common reason a good argument never gets heard.

- 28 C.F.R. 542.14: the formal written request to the Warden is due within 20 calendar days of the event he is complaining about. The regulation does allow an extension where he shows a valid reason for delay, including an extended period in transit away from his documents.
- 28 C.F.R. 542.15: the appeal to the Regional Director is due within 20 calendar days of the date the Warden signed the response, and the appeal to the General Counsel within 30 calendar days of the Regional Director's signature. The General Counsel level "is the final administrative appeal."
- 28 C.F.R. 542.18: the Warden has 20 calendar days to answer, the Regional Director 30, the General Counsel 40. With no answer in time, "the inmate may consider the absence of a response to be a denial at that level." Silence does not stop his clock. He appeals.

Two traps have sunk real petitions. The first is stopping at the Warden. In Herrera v. Rardin the man filed two requests with the Warden about his credits and appealed neither, and the court held he had not properly used the available administrative remedies. The second is rejection on paperwork. In Villegas-Escobar v. Derr, No. 1:22-cv-00500 (D. Haw. Feb. 14, 2023), the regional office never reached the merits. It bounced his appeal for formatting and told him to resubmit with his original request and the complete response attached. He did, months later, and the delay cost him his day in court.

If the prison process is finished and the answer is still wrong, the court route is a petition under 28 U.S.C. 2241, which reaches a person "in custody under or by color of the authority of the United States" who is held "in violation of the Constitution or laws or treaties of the United States." See [what a 2241 petition is](/blog/what-is-a-2241-habeas-petition/) and [what happens after the administrative remedies are finished](/blog/what-happens-after-you-exhaust-administrative-remedies/).

One piece of good news. Finishing the prison process first is not an absolute rule. As the court said in Villegas-Escobar v. Derr: "Exhaustion may be excused if the administrative remedy is inadequate, ineffective, or if attempting to exhaust would be futile or would cause irreparable injury." That is a narrow door, and it is not a reason to skip the filings. It is a reason to document every delay and every bounced form, because that record is the argument.

## Your checklist

- Get the sentence computation sheet and the FSA Time Credit Assessment, in writing, this week.
- Get the Individualized Needs Plan from his last program review and list every completed program with its dates.
- Read his judgment for the exact statute of conviction, and check it against the list in 18 U.S.C. 3632(d)(4)(D).
- Write down every period he was away from programming, and why, with dates.
- Count the thirty day periods yourself and compare your total to his sheet.
- File the informal form, then the written request to the Warden inside 20 calendar days.
- Appeal every denial, and treat silence past the deadline as a denial.
- Keep a dated copy of everything he sends and everything that comes back.
