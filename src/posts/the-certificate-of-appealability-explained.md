Losing a § 2255 motion or a habeas petition feels like the end of the road, but it isn't, at least not procedurally — there's an appeal available. What surprises a lot of people is that you don't get there automatically. Before a federal court of appeals can even look at whether the district court got it wrong, the prisoner has to clear a separate threshold: a certificate of appealability, universally shortened to COA. Without one, the court of appeals doesn't have jurisdiction to decide the merits at all — the case simply doesn't get that far.

## What the statute requires

The requirement comes from 28 U.S.C. § 2253(c), part of the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act. It says a COA may issue "only if the applicant has made a substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right." That phrase — "substantial showing" — is doing real work, and the Supreme Court defined what it means in *Slack v. McDaniel*, 529 U.S. 473 (2000): a prisoner meets the standard by showing that reasonable jurists could debate whether the petition should have been resolved differently, or that the issues presented deserve encouragement to proceed further. It is deliberately not the same as showing you'll actually win on appeal. It's a threshold screening standard, not a merits standard.

*Slack* also addressed a wrinkle that comes up constantly in practice: what happens when the district court denied the petition on procedural grounds — say, timeliness or exhaustion — without ever reaching the underlying constitutional claim. In that situation, the COA inquiry has two separate parts. The prisoner has to show both that jurists of reason would find it debatable whether the petition actually states a valid constitutional claim, *and* that jurists of reason would find it debatable whether the district court's procedural ruling was correct. If there's a plain, correctly applied procedural bar, ordinarily no COA issues — regardless of how strong the underlying constitutional argument might otherwise have been.

## More than nonfrivolous, less than "will probably win"

Later cases have sharpened the distinction between the COA threshold and the ultimate merits. In *Buck v. Davis*, 580 U.S. 100 (2017), the Supreme Court explained that the COA process has two steps: first, the court asks whether the claim is reasonably debatable; if it is, the appeal proceeds in the ordinary course. The Fifth Circuit erred because it effectively decided the merits at the threshold stage and then used that merits determination to deny a COA. The applicant does not have to prove at the COA stage that the appeal will succeed. But the standard still requires more than the bare assertion of a constitutional claim: the applicant must identify a ruling about which reasonable jurists could genuinely disagree.

## Who decides, and why that mechanics question actually matters

The mechanics of who issues a COA have generated real, unresolved disagreement among the circuits. The statute says a "circuit justice or judge" may issue the certificate. Most circuits read that to mean a COA should issue if even one judge on a panel reviewing the request votes to grant it. Other circuits, including at least the Eighth Circuit, allow a panel majority to deny a COA even when one judge on that panel would have granted it. That circuit split matters in practice. Justice Sotomayor addressed the issue in a 2025 dissent from the denial of certiorari — a separate writing that expresses one Justice's view and is not a holding of the Court or binding precedent — observing that a COA grant often triggers appointment of counsel for an indigent prisoner. On that view, the difference between a single-judge standard and a panel-majority standard can be the difference between getting a lawyer for an appeal and not. Because this remains unsettled, the controlling rule is the one in the prisoner's own circuit.

## What denial actually means — and what it doesn't

A district court's denial of a COA is not necessarily the end of the request. The prisoner may seek a COA directly from the court of appeals under the applicable appellate rules. A denial also does not amount to an appellate decision affirming every part of the district court's reasoning. It means that the judge or panel considering the request concluded that the applicant did not make the threshold showing required by § 2253(c) on the issues presented. Because another authorized judge or court may consider a renewed request, the application should identify the specific constitutional issue, the disputed ruling, and the reason reasonable jurists could debate it.

Seeking a COA does not eliminate the need to file a timely notice of appeal; the notice-of-appeal deadline and the COA requirement are separate procedural obligations.

The practical stakes of understanding this system are significant, and they compound with everything else in post-conviction practice: a COA application isn't a formality to rush through, and it isn't the place to throw every conceivable argument at the wall hoping something sticks — courts have specifically criticized "scattershot" COA applications that undermine the statute's screening purpose. The strongest COA request identifies the one or two issues where reasonable jurists genuinely could disagree with the district court, and explains concretely why, rather than restating the entire case.
