Losing a § 2255 motion or a habeas petition feels like the end of the road, but it isn't, at least not procedurally — there's an appeal available. What surprises a lot of people is that you don't get there automatically. Before a federal court of appeals can even look at whether the district court got it wrong, the prisoner has to clear a separate threshold: a certificate of appealability, universally shortened to COA. Without one, the court of appeals doesn't have jurisdiction to decide the merits at all — the case simply doesn't get that far.

## What the statute requires

The requirement comes from 28 U.S.C. § 2253(c), part of the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act. It says a COA may issue "only if the applicant has made a substantial showing of the denial of a constitutional right." That phrase — "substantial showing" — is doing real work, and the Supreme Court defined what it means in *Slack v. McDaniel*, 529 U.S. 473 (2000): a prisoner meets the standard by showing that reasonable jurists could debate whether the petition should have been resolved differently, or that the issues presented deserve encouragement to proceed further. It is deliberately not the same as showing you'll actually win on appeal. It's a threshold screening standard, not a merits standard.

*Slack* also addressed a wrinkle that comes up constantly in practice: what happens when the district court denied the petition on procedural grounds — say, timeliness or exhaustion — without ever reaching the underlying constitutional claim. In that situation, the COA inquiry has two separate parts. The prisoner has to show both that jurists of reason would find it debatable whether the petition actually states a valid constitutional claim, *and* that jurists of reason would find it debatable whether the district court's procedural ruling was correct. If there's a plain, correctly applied procedural bar, no COA issues — the case is over regardless of how strong the underlying constitutional argument might otherwise have been.

## More than nonfrivolous, less than "will probably win"

Later cases have sharpened this further. In *Buck v. Davis*, 580 U.S. 100 (2017), the Court reiterated that meeting the COA standard requires more than showing a lack of frivolousness or a good-faith argument — but it stopped well short of requiring the petitioner to demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success. The Court has also repeatedly warned lower courts against treating COA review too casually or too harshly in either direction. In *Buck*, the Court found that a Fifth Circuit-style rushed, unreasoned COA denial can itself be reversible error — COA review is meant to be a genuine, if limited, threshold look, not a rubber stamp against relief, particularly for pro se litigants navigating this alone.

## Who decides, and why that mechanics question actually matters

The mechanics of who issues a COA have generated real, unresolved disagreement among the circuits. The statute says a "circuit justice or judge" may issue the certificate. Most circuits read that to mean a COA should issue if even one judge on a panel reviewing the request votes to grant it. Other circuits, including at least the Eighth Circuit, allow a panel majority to deny a COA even when one judge on that panel would have granted it. That circuit split matters enormously in practice — as Justice Sotomayor pointed out in a 2025 dissent from denial of certiorari in a case presenting exactly this issue, a COA grant often triggers appointment of counsel for an indigent prisoner, meaning the difference between a single-judge standard and a panel-majority standard can be the difference between getting a lawyer for your appeal and not.

## What denial actually means — and what it doesn't

A COA denial isn't necessarily the end of every road. If the district court denies a COA, the prisoner can renew the request directly with the court of appeals. And a COA denial doesn't retroactively validate the district court's reasoning on the merits — it simply means no reasonable jurist would currently debate the outcome, based on the record as it stood. That's a real, meaningful bar, but it's also a narrower one than "the district court was correct."

The practical stakes of understanding this system are significant, and they compound with everything else in post-conviction practice: a COA application isn't a formality to rush through, and it isn't the place to throw every conceivable argument at the wall hoping something sticks — courts have specifically criticized "scattershot" COA applications that undermine the statute's screening purpose. The strongest COA request identifies the one or two issues where reasonable jurists genuinely could disagree with the district court, and explains concretely why, rather than restating the entire case.
