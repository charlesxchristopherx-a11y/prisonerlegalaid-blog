On May 28, 2026, the Federal Bureau of Prisons announced a change that affects how a lot of people get from prison to home. The policy is called "Strategic Expansion of Minimum-Security Camp Utilization," and the short version is this: instead of going straight from your current institution to a halfway house or home confinement once you're approved, many eligible people will now stop first at a minimum-security Federal Prison Camp. Here's what that actually means, answered in the order families usually ask it.

**Who qualifies for this?** Anyone who has already received an approved Residential Reentry Center (RRC) placement date or a Home Confinement placement date, and who meets the security and custody criteria for camp placement under Program Statement 5100.08 CN-3, Inmate Security Designation and Custody Classification. The BOP has said the policy applies to approved individuals across security levels, but — and this matters — initial implementation is prioritizing people currently housed in low-security institutions who are appropriate for camp placement. If you're at a higher security level, you may see this policy applied to your situation later rather than right away.

**Who's excluded?** The BOP's announcement lists specific categories that don't qualify for this camp transfer regardless of everything else: sex offenders, deportable aliens, Disruptive Group members, domestic or international terrorists, inmates requiring heightened monitoring, inmates with recent serious misconduct, and — a catch-all — any case where placement would pose an identifiable risk to public safety. If any of these apply, this particular transfer isn't happening, though your existing RRC or home confinement placement itself isn't necessarily affected.

**Will this speed up your release date?** No, and this is the most important thing to understand. This policy does not move up your release date, your RRC date, or your home confinement date by a single day. What it changes is where you're physically housed in the time between now and that date — at a minimum-security camp instead of staying at your current institution until you report directly to the RRC or home confinement. The BOP's stated reasoning is that a camp offers a more gradual, community-like transition than moving straight from a higher-security setting, and it makes better use of camp capacity that's often underused. Whether that framing lands as a genuine benefit or as extra transportation and disruption before release will vary person to person — but either way, the timeline to your actual release doesn't change.

**Does every eligible person with an RRC date get transferred?** Not automatically, and not necessarily right away. The announcement describes this as something institutions "will begin" doing, with low-security facilities prioritized first. If you're eligible on paper, it's still worth confirming with your case manager whether and when your specific transfer is being processed, rather than assuming it will happen the moment your RRC date is approved.

**What should you expect once a transfer is approved?** A move to a different facility — the camp — sometime between your approval and your original RRC or home confinement reporting date. Because these transfers still run through the standard classification process under PS 5100.08 CN-3, expect the same kind of paperwork and timing uncertainty that accompanies any BOP transfer: it can happen faster than expected, or slower, and specific dates are rarely locked in far in advance. It's worth knowing this is coming so a sudden transfer notice doesn't catch your family off guard.

**How does this connect to the existing RRC planning process?** It doesn't replace it. Unit teams still begin RRC placement planning roughly 17 to 19 months before an expected release date, weighing the same five factors under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b) they always have. This camp-transfer policy is a layer added on top of that existing timeline — an intermediate stop for eligible people, not a change to when RRC planning starts or how placement length gets decided.

If you're trying to figure out whether this policy applies to a specific situation — especially where the eligibility categories aren't entirely clear-cut — that's exactly the kind of question worth raising with the unit team directly, backed up with the actual policy language rather than secondhand descriptions of it.

Separately from this policy, if the underlying problem is simply distance — the facility is hundreds of miles from family — that's a different request with its own statutory basis. Section 601 of the First Step Act directs the BOP to place people within 500 driving miles of their primary residence where practicable.
