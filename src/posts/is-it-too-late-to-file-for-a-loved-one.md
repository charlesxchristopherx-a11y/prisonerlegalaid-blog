Someone has told you the year is up and there is nothing left to do. Before you accept that, go find the dates. The one year limit on a post conviction filing does not have one start date. It has four, and the latest of them governs. Most families who are told it is too late have never checked which one applies.

Here is what to do this week, and then what the law actually says.

## What you can do this week

You do not have to understand any of the law below to start. You need paper.

Get the docket sheet from the court that sentenced him. It shows the judgment date and every filing in the case. Federal dockets come through PACER, and the clerk of court can tell you what a paper copy costs. Here is [how to pull his docket without running up a bill](/checklists/pacer-records/).

Find the date the appeal ended. If there was no appeal, find the date the time to appeal ran out. Most start date questions turn on that one line.

List everything already filed on his behalf, with the date each one was filed and the date it was decided. Those dates change the arithmetic, and nobody can reconstruct them later as well as you can now.

Then write down what he was never able to do, and why. Records he asked for and did not get. Law library access he was denied. Mail that vanished. Stretches when he was physically unable to work on his case. Put down dates, names and what he asked for. Send him a written request asking him to do the same from his side, keep a copy, and use certified mail if you are sending anything to the prison. This list is the raw material for two of the four start dates, and it is the part families almost never have.

If you want someone to look at the dates with you, [request a free case review](/#contact).

## Start with which court convicted him

Everything hangs on this, and it takes one minute to answer.

If he was convicted in federal court, his filing is a motion under **28 U.S.C. 2255(f)**, made in the same court that sentenced him. If he was convicted in state court, his federal petition is under **28 U.S.C. 2254**, and its clock comes from **28 U.S.C. 2244(d)**. He may also have state post conviction remedies with their own separate deadlines, which are not the same from state to state. Our [2254 guide](/topics/2254-habeas/) covers that route.

Both routes carry one year. They are not the same year, and mixing them up is the most common mistake we see.

## What the one year runs from

For a federal conviction, the statute says a one year period of limitation applies and that "[t]he limitation period shall run from the latest of" four dates. In the statute's own words, those are:

1. the date on which "the judgment of conviction becomes final";
2. the date on which "the impediment to making a motion created by governmental action in violation of the Constitution or laws of the United States is removed, if the movant was prevented from making a motion by such governmental action";
3. the date on which the right asserted "was initially recognized by the Supreme Court, if that right has been newly recognized by the Supreme Court and made retroactively applicable to cases on collateral review"; or
4. the date on which "the facts supporting the claim or claims presented could have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence".

Read the second one twice. That is the one families never hear about. If the facility withheld his records, blocked his access to the law library or lost his legal mail, and that is what kept him from filing, the start date may not be where everyone assumed.

The third one is narrower than it sounds. A new decision moves the clock only if the Supreme Court made the right retroactively applicable on collateral review. Most new rules are not. A case in the news is not by itself a new deadline.

The fourth one is stricter than it sounds. It does not say when he found out. It says when the facts "could have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence". That is often an earlier date, and the gap between the two is where filings are lost.

For a state conviction, 28 U.S.C. 2244(d) sets out a parallel set of four start dates: finality at the end of direct review or when the time for seeking it expires, removal of an impediment created by unlawful State action, a newly recognized right made retroactively applicable, and the date the factual predicate "could have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence".

## What a pending state case does to the clock

You will read that a pending state post conviction case stops the federal deadline. That is close, and the difference matters.

The statute says "[t]he time during which a properly filed application for State post conviction or other collateral review with respect to the pertinent judgment or claim is pending shall not be counted toward any period of limitation under this subsection." It pauses the count. It does not restart it, and it does not give back time already spent. If ten months of his year were used before the state petition went in, two months are available when the state case ends. Not twelve. Families who believe otherwise wait, and the wait is the bar.

Two more things about that provision. "Properly filed" is doing real work: an application that was not properly filed pauses nothing, and that is usually discovered after the year is gone. And a direct appeal is a different mechanism. During direct review the one year has not started yet, so there is nothing to pause.

## If the year has already run, two doors stay open

Neither is easy. Both are real.

**Equitable tolling.** In *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631 (2010), the Supreme Court held that a petitioner is entitled to [equitable tolling](/blog/equitable-tolling-prison-mail-delays-2255-deadline/) only if he shows "(1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way" and prevented timely filing. Both halves are required, and the first is where most attempts fail. Diligence is a record he either has or does not have, which is why the list you write this week matters so much. Be clear about the limit: the Court described "a garden variety claim of excusable neglect" as the kind of thing that does not warrant equitable tolling. Not understanding the deadline is not, by itself, an excuse for missing it.

**A credible showing that he is innocent.** In *McQuiggin v. Perkins*, 569 U.S. 383 (2013), the Supreme Court held that "actual innocence, if proved, serves as a gateway through which a petitioner may pass" even when the obstacle is an expired one year limit rather than a procedural bar. Our [guide to gateway innocence claims](/blog/actual-innocence-gateway-claims-schlup-v-delo/) walks through what that takes. The Court was blunt about how rare this is: "tenable actual-innocence gateway pleas are rare," and a petitioner "does not meet the threshold requirement unless he persuades the district court that, in light of the new evidence, no juror, acting reasonably, would have voted to find him guilty beyond a reasonable doubt." That means new evidence, not a retelling of the trial. An expired clock is still not automatically the end of it.

## The traps that cost families the most

- Assuming the year started on the sentencing date. It starts at the latest of the four dates in **28 U.S.C. 2255(f)**, and finality is only the first of them.
- Believing a new filing buys a fresh year. It does not.
- Waiting for a state case to finish before counting. The count was already running before it started.
- Relying on a new Supreme Court decision without checking whether it was made retroactively applicable on collateral review.
- Letting the record of what he asked for and never got go cold. That record is the start date argument and the diligence argument, and it fades fast.

## Your checklist

1. Order the docket sheet from the sentencing court this week.
2. Write down the date the appeal ended, or the date the time to appeal expired.
3. List every post conviction filing with its filing date and decision date.
4. Build the written record of records requests, law library denials and lost mail, with dates and names.
5. Ask him to send you his own dated account of the same events, and keep a copy.
6. Check whether any decision he is relying on was made retroactively applicable on collateral review.
7. Do it now rather than when you feel ready. Files get purged and staff transfer out.

Start with the docket sheet. Once you have the dates, somebody can tell you which of the four start dates applies to him, and that is a different conversation from the one you were handed.
