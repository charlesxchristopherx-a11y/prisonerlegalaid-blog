## He signs it. You can still build it.

The motion itself has to be his. 28 U.S.C. § 2255 gives the right to "a prisoner in custody under sentence of a court established by Act of Congress," and it is that prisoner who "may move the court which imposed the sentence to vacate, set aside or correct the sentence." His name, his signature, his filing.

Most families hear that and conclude there is nothing left for them to do. That conclusion costs people their cases. A judge decides most [section 2255 motions](/topics/2255-motions/) by reading paper, and almost every piece of that paper is something you can get from the outside while he cannot. The transcripts. His trial lawyer's file. A signed page from the cousin who sat in the lawyer's office and heard what was said. The rules written for these cases let a judge take affidavits into the record, which means a statement you collect this month is something a judge can read next year.

Start this week. The slowest pieces are the ones other people have to send you.

## What you can gather this week

**The transcripts.** Write to the clerk of the court that sentenced him and ask for the transcript of the trial or of the plea hearing, and the transcript of the sentencing. Give the case number and the judge's name. Transcripts cost money, which stops many families here, so know this: 28 U.S.C. § 2250 says that where an order has been made permitting a petitioner to proceed without prepaying, "the clerk of any court of the United States shall furnish to the petitioner without cost certified copies of such documents or parts of the record on file in his office as may be required by order of the judge before whom the application is pending." That runs through the judge, so the request has to be made, in writing.

**His trial lawyer's file.** Write to the lawyer who handled the case and ask for the complete file: the plea agreement, what the government turned over, the letters, the notes, any investigator's reports. Put it in writing even if you have his phone number, and send it by certified mail so there is a date on it.

**His own account, signed.** Have him write out what happened in his own words and sign it. Not conclusions. Specifics. What was said, by whom, on what date, in what room, and who else was present.

**Statements from anyone else who was there.** A short signed page from one person who heard a promise, or who was never contacted by the defense before trial, is worth more than a long letter about how unfair it all felt.

Send everything by certified mail and keep a copy of every single thing you send. If you want someone to look at what you have put together, [request a free case review](/#contact).

## What the law actually requires

The motion has to say the conviction or the sentence was unlawful, not merely unfair. The statute lists the grounds: that the sentence "was imposed in violation of the Constitution or laws of the United States," that the court "was without jurisdiction to impose such sentence," that it "was in excess of the maximum authorized by law," or that it "is otherwise subject to collateral attack."

The most common ground is his own lawyer. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984) requires two things, and both have to be there. First, the defendant must show that counsel's work "fell below an objective standard of reasonableness." Second, "the defendant must show that there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different." Courts start from the assumption that the lawyer made a reasonable professional choice, which is [what the Strickland standard asks of him](/blog/ineffective-assistance-of-counsel-strickland-standard/) and why your detail matters.

Staying quiet about it on appeal does not kill this kind of claim, which matters if [his appeal is already finished](/blog/husband-lost-his-appeal-is-it-over/). Massaro v. United States, 538 U.S. 500 (2003) holds that "an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may be brought in a collateral proceeding under § 2255, whether or not the petitioner could have raised the claim on direct appeal."

## Why the paper decides it, and what that gives you

28 U.S.C. § 2255(b) says that "unless the motion and the files and records of the case conclusively show that the prisoner is entitled to no relief," the court "shall cause notice thereof to be served upon the United States attorney, grant a prompt hearing thereon, determine the issues and make findings of fact and conclusions of law with respect thereto." So the first fight is not about winning. It is about whether the papers leave a real question open.

Two of the rules for these cases turn that into work you can do.

Rule 6 of the Rules Governing Section 2255 Proceedings is the hard half. He cannot simply demand documents from the government: "A judge may, for good cause, authorize a party to conduct discovery," and "a party requesting discovery must provide reasons for the request." Permission first, reasons first. Nothing arrives automatically.

Rule 7 is the half that gives you a way in. The judge "may direct the parties to expand the record by submitting additional materials relating to the motion," and those materials "include letters predating the filing of the motion, documents, exhibits, and answers under oath to written interrogatories propounded by the judge." Then the sentence worth copying down: "Affidavits also may be submitted and considered as part of the record." Rule 8 closes the loop, because the judge "must review the answer, any transcripts and records of prior proceedings, and any materials submitted under Rule 7 to determine whether an evidentiary hearing is warranted." The statements you collect are in the pile the judge reads.

If it reaches a hearing, he is not alone for it. Rule 8 says that where a hearing is warranted "the judge must appoint an attorney to represent a moving party who qualifies to have counsel appointed."

What the paper has to carry is detail. Blackledge v. Allison, 431 U.S. 63 (1977) is blunt about the weight of what he said in court at the time: "Solemn declarations in open court carry a strong presumption of verity." That same case is why the story is not over: the Court would not adopt a rule shutting out every possibility that what he said in court was the product of "misunderstanding, duress, or misrepresentation by others." What got that petitioner a hearing was specificity. The Court noted that his petition "indicated exactly what the terms of the promise were; when, where, and by whom the promise had been made; and the identity of one witness to its communication." Four things. Use them as the outline for every statement you collect.

## Deadlines and traps

The clock is the first thing to pin down. 28 U.S.C. § 2255 sets "a 1-year period of limitation," running from the latest of four dates. The one that applies in most cases is "the date on which the judgment of conviction becomes final." The others are the date an "impediment to making a motion created by governmental action" is removed, the date a right "newly recognized by the Supreme Court" was initially recognized if it was also "made retroactively applicable to cases on collateral review," and "the date on which the facts supporting the claim or claims presented could have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence." Write that date down where you will find it again.

Nobody is handed a lawyer to write the motion. 28 U.S.C. § 2255(g) says the court "may appoint counsel." May, not shall. Plan on the motion being prepared without one.

In practice he gets one real attempt, so the first filing has to carry every ground he has. A second or later motion must be certified by a panel of the court of appeals, and 28 U.S.C. § 2255(h) allows that only for "newly discovered evidence" strong enough that "no reasonable factfinder would have found the movant guilty of the offense," or for "a new rule of constitutional law, made retroactive to cases on collateral review by the Supreme Court, that was previously unavailable." If he is telling you [he did not do it](/blog/my-son-says-he-is-innocent/), that belongs in the first filing with everything else, not held back for later.

## Your checklist

- The date his judgment of conviction became final, written down.
- Written request to the clerk of the sentencing court for the trial or plea transcript and the sentencing transcript, with the case number.
- Written request to his trial lawyer for the complete file, sent by certified mail.
- His own signed account: the words, the date, the place, who else was there.
- A signed statement from each other person who heard or saw something.
- A copy of every letter you send and every reply you get.
- One page listing every ground he thinks he has, so nothing is left out of the first motion.
