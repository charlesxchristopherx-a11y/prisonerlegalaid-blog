Procedural default is one of the most common reasons a genuinely strong claim never gets heard in federal habeas review. If a state court refused to consider your claim because you didn't follow the state's own procedural rules, a federal court will usually refuse to consider it too — unless you can show "cause" for the default and resulting prejudice. For a specific, narrow category of claims, *Martinez v. Ryan* changed what counts as cause.

## The general rule Martinez had to work around

Before *Martinez*, the controlling rule (from *Coleman v. Thompson*) was blunt: a postconviction attorney's negligence generally does not count as "cause" to excuse a procedural default, because the attorney is legally your agent — under ordinary agency principles, you bear the consequences of your own representative's mistakes, even in a collateral proceeding where you have no constitutional right to counsel at all.

## The narrow exception Martinez created

*Martinez v. Ryan*, 566 U.S. 1 (2012), carved out a specific, equitable exception to that rule. In states where the law *requires* an ineffective-assistance-of-trial-counsel claim to be raised for the first time in an initial-review collateral proceeding — rather than on direct appeal — the Supreme Court held that **inadequate assistance of counsel in that specific initial-review proceeding can itself establish cause** to excuse a later procedural default of the underlying trial-counsel claim.

The Court's reasoning was practical, not constitutional: if the *only* place state law lets you raise a trial-ineffectiveness claim is a collateral proceeding, and the lawyer handling that one shot at raising it fails you, then as a practical matter no court at any level ever actually considered the claim. The Court found that gap unacceptable enough to justify a narrow, equity-based fix — while being explicit that it was *not* creating a constitutional right to effective counsel in collateral proceedings generally.

## The specific conditions that have to be met

*Martinez* applies only when **both** of these are true:

1. Either **no counsel was appointed** for you in that initial-review collateral proceeding, **or** the counsel who was appointed was **ineffective under the *Strickland* standard**; and
2. The underlying ineffective-assistance-of-trial-counsel claim is **"substantial"** — meaning it has to actually have some merit, not just be raised.

## Where the exception stops — and this is where people get it wrong

*Martinez* is deliberately narrow, and the Court was explicit about its limits:

- It applies **only to the first, initial-review collateral proceeding** — not to an appeal from that proceeding, and not to a second or successive collateral proceeding.
- It applies **only to claims of ineffective assistance at trial** — not to other defaulted constitutional claims.
- It applies **only in states that structurally require** trial-IAC claims to be raised in a collateral proceeding rather than on direct appeal. If your state lets you raise ineffective-assistance-of-trial-counsel claims on direct appeal, *Martinez* generally doesn't apply, because the structural gap it was designed to fix isn't present.
- It does not, by itself, entitle you to habeas relief. Establishing "cause and prejudice" under *Martinez* only opens the door for a federal court to consider the merits of the underlying claim — it doesn't decide the claim in your favor.

Getting the *Martinez* analysis right starts with knowing your own state's procedural rules on where an ineffective-assistance claim is supposed to be raised in the first place — because whether the exception is even available at all turns entirely on that structural question.
